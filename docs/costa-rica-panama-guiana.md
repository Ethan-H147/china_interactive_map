# Costa Rica, Panama and the Guiana frontier

Added locally on 25 October 2026. The maps use the shared regional renderer, scoped subdivision chunks, Spanish names and aliases, national flag viewer, saved views and first-level quizzes. Population and economic figures use separately retained statistical sources with their own years and territorial definitions. Area cards show calculated land area for the stated boundary snapshot.

Costa Rica contains seven provinces, 84 cantons and 492 districts from the retained official IGN administrative geography published by ARESEP. This is dated geometry: the IGN's 2026 register lists 494 districts, so the newer division is outside this snapshot. The newer SNIT service was unavailable during retrieval; older mirrors were checked against the retained roster. Isla del Coco remains a district under Puntarenas, with a separate focused island entry and searchable “Cocos Island” alias. Original codes and accents are retained.

Panama uses ANATI / Instituto Geográfico Nacional Tommy Guardia's hosted CORREGIMIENTOS data, matching the official 2025 DPA edition with a 2024 boundary reference. All 699 published land areas are represented; the 31 source water features remain water. The source groups these into ten provinces and three province-level comarcas. Its district layer includes a cartographic placeholder for Kuna Yala; the atlas omits that invented district and attaches the four real corregimientos directly to their comarca. Naso Tjër Di keeps the source's district-level comarca classification. ANATI explicitly states that Naso's three internal corregimiento limits have inconsistencies and are not included in the data, so they are not fabricated here. Its whole-area source row maps to the real comarca record.

The resulting Panamanian map has 13 first-level units, 86 second-level units (districts, Naso and four direct corregimientos), and 694 third-level corregimientos. Search includes both Guna Yala and the source spelling Kuna Yala. The source roster-to-display mapping is retained in `scripts/boundary-sources/costa-rica-panama/display-mapping.json`.

Both countries' parent outlines are dissolved from the same detailed leaves. Panama follows Colombia's existing detailed frontier; small self-intersection cleanup has a maximum 12-metre validation allowance there. Costa Rica follows Panama's retained edge with a three-metre allowance. Nicaragua's adjacent gray silhouette follows Costa Rica inland; the river delta has a five-kilometre coastal endpoint allowance to preserve its real water. Existing interactive-country fallback geometries retain their exact prior bytes. The former gray Costa Rica and Panama silhouettes and their obsolete background strokes are removed, and their contexts, compact copies, border previews and globe overview are refreshed.

Source metadata, hashes, download URLs, boundary dates and flag credits are retained beside the input geometry. Run `npm run fetch:costa-rica-panama` to verify or restore the pinned files, `npm run build:costa-rica-panama` to rebuild, and `npm run test:costa-rica-panama` for data and desktop/mobile checks. An upstream hash change requires source review rather than silently replacing the snapshot.

The Brazil–French Guiana double border came from two independent stroke sources: France's river outline and a Brazil-owned global copy. France now owns one complete 2,114-vertex path. The repair removes 1,291 duplicate Brazilian segments and replaces 2,089 fragmented French segments. The surviving Brazil–Suriname stroke joins the French source's rainforest tripoint, resolving a roughly 1.9-kilometre difference between surveys. Administrative land geometry is unchanged.

`dist/data/guiana-frontier.json` retains the shared path, old Brazilian arc and preservation hashes. `npm run build:guiana-frontier` rebuilds the stroke and its derived assets; the global frontier builder reapplies the ownership repair, and the Europe build finishes with it. `npm run test:guiana-frontier` verifies complete coverage, absence of the second stroke, unchanged land and one visible owner in France and Brazil, on desktop and phone in both projections.

## Statistical coverage

The retained statistical workbooks and normalized rows live in `scripts/statistics-sources/costa-rica-panama/`. The manifest pins both original and normalized bytes. `scripts/statistics-sources-extract.py` documents the extraction (openpyxl and xlrd 2.0.2). Normal builds read the retained JSON without a Python dependency. Run `npm run build:costa-rica-panama-statistics` to refresh cards and colours offline, and `npm run test:costa-rica-panama-statistics` for numerical and desktop/mobile checks. The full geographic build reapplies these statistics after rebuilding mapped areas.

Costa Rica’s population workbook has 82 legacy cantons and 487 districts. The whole Monteverde and Puerto Jiménez district estimates are reassigned to their later cantons and subtracted from Puntarenas and Golfito respectively. All 84 current canton counts sum to their province totals; the seven provinces sum to 5,044,197. District populations in Paraíso, Jiménez, Barva and Garabito remain unavailable because the table predates their newer district divisions. Isla del Coco has no matching population row; its missing value is not converted to zero. BCCR’s public PIB Cantonal report provides 2022 current-price GDP and published GDP per capita for all 84 current cantons. Both exported measures are in millions of colones, so both are multiplied by one million before display; the matching 2022 annual exchange rate converts them to USD. Provincial GDP sums constituent canton output; provincial per capita divides that sum by the 2022 INEC population estimate. It does not average canton per-capita figures. The original public export and selected 2022 slicer state are retained.

Panama’s province counts sum to 4,064,780. City and “rest of district” subtotals are skipped without changing the enclosing district; La Chorrera’s same-name city subtotal is specifically guarded against replacing its district total. Explicit spelling aliases are qualified by the containing province/district. The missing Naso count is not inferred from El Teribe. Changuinola, Las Delicias, Bajo Culubre, Zapallal and La Colorada have material territorial discrepancies between the census areas and retained map; their population is withheld pending a verified crosswalk. Every exclusion is recorded in `coverage.json` and explained in its card.

Panamanian GDP Cuadro 19 is current-price output in millions of balboas, not the workbook’s earlier chained-volume table. Column 2024 (E) remains labeled estimated. Only Coclé, Herrera, Los Santos, Panamá and Panamá Oeste have exact map scopes. Bocas del Toro, Chiriquí and Veraguas include allocated Ngäbe Buglé territory; Colón includes Kuna Yala; Darién includes Emberá. Their combined statistical-region totals are shown separately as explicitly labeled More figures on the relevant province cards and excluded from province GDP colours. Published per-capita figures use the INEC midyear 2024 projections based on the 2010 census, as specified in workbook footnote (4). Their denominator is not reconstructed from the GDP/per-capita ratio. National 2025 per-capita figures likewise use the World Bank’s matching-year population, never the earlier population card. No GDP is inherited by smaller units. Map colours retain missing values and use GDP only where territorial scope matches.
